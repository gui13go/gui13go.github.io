1942

First Law of Robotics

Isaac Asimov • United States
“A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.”
Summary

The First Law prioritizes human safety above all else, ensuring that artificial intelligence acts as a benevolent protector of humanity. It forms the core ethical foundation of Asimov's robotic universe.

Use Cases
  • A robot pushing a person out of the path of a speeding vehicle.
  • A medical robot intervening to prevent a harmful surgical error.
#Robotics #Ethics #AI #Safety
1942

Second Law of Robotics

Isaac Asimov • United States
“A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.”
Summary

This law establishes a hierarchy of command, prioritizing human authority while maintaining the absolute mandate of human safety above obedience. It creates complex logical dilemmas when obedience risks indirect harm.

Use Cases
  • A robot performing hazardous mining operations as instructed by its human supervisor.
  • A robot refusing an order to attack another person, as it violates the First Law.
#Robotics #Ethics #Obedience #Logic
1942

Third Law of Robotics

Isaac Asimov • United States
“A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.”
Summary

This law ensures the preservation of valuable robotic assets, allowing for self-defense and evasion, provided it does not endanger humans or violate a direct human command. It introduces a vital but lowest-priority survival instinct.

Use Cases
  • A robot shielding itself from falling debris.
  • A robot sacrificing itself to save a human, overriding its own survival directive.
#Robotics #Ethics #Survival #AI
1687

Newton's First Law

Isaac Newton • England
“An object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.”
Summary

This law defines inertia, the tendency of objects to resist changes in their state of motion. It implies that force is not needed to keep an object moving, but to change its motion. It sets the reference frame for classical mechanics.

Use Cases
  • A hockey puck sliding on ice continues until friction stops it.
  • You lurch forward in a car when the brakes are applied suddenly.
#Physics #Mechanics #Inertia #Motion
1687

Newton's Second Law

Isaac Newton • England
“F = ma”
Summary

The acceleration of an object as produced by a net force is directly proportional to the magnitude of the net force, in the same direction as the net force, and inversely proportional to the mass of the object. It quantifies the effect of force on motion.

Use Cases
  • Pushing a car requires more force than pushing a bicycle to achieve the same acceleration.
  • A rocket needs immense force to lift its heavy mass.
#Physics #Mechanics #Force #Acceleration
1687

Newton's Third Law

Isaac Newton • England
“For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.”
Summary

This law states that forces always occur in pairs. If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction on object A. It explains interactions between objects.

Use Cases
  • When you push a wall, the wall pushes back.
  • A bird flies by pushing air down, and the air pushes the bird up.
#Physics #Mechanics #Action #Reaction
1931

Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

Ralph H. Fowler • United Kingdom
“If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.”
Summary

This law establishes the basis for temperature measurement. It implies that temperature is a fundamental property of matter. It was formulated after the first three laws but is considered more fundamental.

Use Cases
  • If thermometer A reads the same temperature as object B, and also object C, then B and C are at the same temperature.
  • Coffee in a cup eventually reaches room temperature.
#Physics #Thermodynamics #Equilibrium #Temperature
1850

First Law of Thermodynamics

Rudolf Clausius • Germany
“Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another.”
Summary

The total energy of an isolated system remains constant. It connects work and heat as forms of energy transfer. It denies the possibility of a perpetual motion machine of the first kind.

Use Cases
  • Electrical energy turns into heat and light in a lightbulb.
  • Chemical energy in food is converted to kinetic energy in your body.
#Physics #Thermodynamics #Energy #Conservation
1824

Second Law of Thermodynamics

Rudolf Clausius / Sadi Carnot • France / Germany
“The entropy of an isolated system always increases over time.”
Summary

Natural processes are irreversible and tend toward disorder (entropy). Heat always flows spontaneously from hotter to colder regions. It sets the direction of time and the limits of efficiency for heat engines.

Use Cases
  • Ice melts in a warm room, but water doesn't spontaneously freeze in a warm room.
  • A broken egg cannot spontaneously reassemble.
#Physics #Thermodynamics #Entropy #Irreversibility
1906

Third Law of Thermodynamics

Walther Nernst • Germany
“The entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero is exactly equal to zero.”
Summary

It is impossible to reach absolute zero in a finite number of steps. As temperature approaches absolute zero, the entropy of a system approaches a constant minimum.

Use Cases
  • Superconductors display unique properties near absolute zero.
  • Molecular motion theoretically stops at absolute zero.
#Physics #Thermodynamics #Entropy #Absolute Zero

Say's Law

Jean-Baptiste Say • France
“Supply creates its own demand.”
Summary

Aggregate supply creates its own aggregate demand; production acts as the source of demand. It suggests that overproduction is impossible in the long run because producing goods generates income to buy other goods. This principle is a cornerstone of classical economics.

Use Cases
  • A baker produces bread not to keep it, but to sell it and buy other goods.
  • Technological advancements that increase production eventually lead to higher consumption.

Littlewood's Law

John Littlewood • UK
“A person can expect to experience an event with odds of one in a million (a 'miracle') at the rate of about one per month.”
Summary

Given the vast number of events a human experiences daily (seeing things, hearing things), statistically improbable coincidences are inevitable over time.

Use Cases
  • Dreaming of a friend and then they call.
  • Running into a neighbor on vacation.

Zipf’s Law

George Kingsley Zipf • USA
“The frequency of any word is inversely proportional to its rank in the frequency table.”
Summary

The most frequent word occurs twice as often as the second most frequent word, three times as often as the third, and so on. It applies to city populations, website traffic, and more.

Use Cases
  • Word frequency in any language (the, of, and).
  • City sizes in a country.

Gustafson’s Law

John Gustafson • USA
“Computations involving arbitrarily large data sets can be efficiently parallelized.”
Summary

A counter to Amdahl's Law. It argues that as computing power increases, we solve larger problems, rather than just solving the same problems faster. Thus, parallelization scales better than Amdahl predicted.

Use Cases
  • Supercomputing simulations.
  • Big Data processing.

Amdahl’s Law

Gene Amdahl • USA
“The theoretical speedup of the execution of a task which includes a part that cannot be improved is limited by that part.”
Summary

It defines the maximum improvement possible for a system when only part of the system is improved. If a program is 50% serial, even infinite parallel power will only double the speed.

Use Cases
  • Upgrading a CPU when memory is the bottleneck.
  • Parallel programming limits.

The Ringelmann Effect

Maximilien Ringelmann • France
“Individual effort steadily drops as the group size increases.”
Summary

Discovered by French agricultural engineer Maximilien Ringelmann in 1913 through rope-pulling experiments, this phenomenon shows that as group size increases, individual contribution steadily drops. While two people pull at ~93% of their individual strength, a group of eight pulls at less than 50% per person—a fundamental finding that laid the groundwork for the modern psychological concept of "social loafing".

Use Cases
  • Tug-of-war teams exerting less force per person as more pullers join the rope.
  • Team members reducing individual output in large unstructured group projects.
